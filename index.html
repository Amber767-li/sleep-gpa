Sleep time and GPA

Author

Yunjun Li Amber

Introduction and literature review

For decades, the relationship between sleep and cognitive function has been a popular topic. Well-controlled sleep research conducted with healthy adults has found that better sleep is related to a myriad of superior cognitive functions, including better learning(Diekelmann et al., 2009; Okano et al., 2019) and memory(Fogel et al., 2007). These effects have been found to extend beyond the laboratory setting, such that self-reported sleep measures from students in the comfort of their own homes have also been found to be associated with academic performance(Eliasson et al., 2010; Gaultney, 2010).

Sleep is thought to play an important and specific role in memory consolidation. Even though the exact underlying mechanisms of the relationship between sleep, memory, and neuroplasticity are yet to be fully explored, it is widely believed that specific synaptic connections that were active during awake periods are strengthened during sleep, facilitating the consolidation of memory (Gilestro et al., 2009; Walker & Stickgold, 2006). Therefore, sleep provides a crucial function for allowing us to remember what has been studied, which in turn is essential for successful academic performance.

Apart from the influence of sleep on memory consolidation, lack of sleep has been linked to poor attention and cognition. Well-controlled sleep deprivation studies have shown that lack of sleep not only increases fatigue and sleepiness but also worsens cognitive performance(Harrison & Horne, 2000; Lim & Dinges, 2010). Interestingly, previous research found that the cognitive performance of an individual who has been awake for 17 hours is equivalent to that exhibited by one who has a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%(Dawson & Reid, 1997). Outside of a laboratory setting, studies examining sleep in the comfort of people’s own homes via self-report surveys have found that, compared with persistently good sleepers persistently poor sleepers experience significantly more daytime difficulies in regards to fatigue, sleepiness, and poor cognition (Alapin et al., 2000).

Sleep is associated with academic performance in school. Sleep deficit has been associated with decreased concentration and attention during class(Orzech et al., 2011). More importantly, the timing of sleep may be particularly relevant to academic performance. In a questionnaire study, Students with higher academic performance reported earlier bedtimes and wake times than students with lower performance(Eliasson et al., 2010). Additionally, under a fixed bedtime could reduce the time available for sleep, making shorter sleep duration one possible explanation for an association between later bedtime and lower GPA.

Taken all together, these studies motivate examining bedtime and sleep duration jointly. The present study used an existing dataset (Onyper et al., 2012) to examine the relationship between average weekday bedtime and GPA among college students.

The present study addressed four research questions:

  1. Is average weekday bedtime associated with GPA?
  2. Does a quadratic term improve the description of the bedtime–GPA association beyond a linear model?
  3. Does the bedtime–GPA association change after accounting for weekday sleep duration?
  4. Is there an indirect statistical association between bedtime and GPA involving weekday sleep duration?

Method

Research Design and Participants

The present study involved a secondary analysis of the SleepStudy dataset, which contains data from 253 college students and identifies Onyper et al. (2012) as its original source. The original data collection included questionnaires about students’ attitudes and habits, cognitive assessments, and sleep diaries maintained over a two-week period. The present analysis focused on three variables: GPA, average weekday bedtime, and average weekday sleep duration. No new participants were recruited, and sleep schedules were not experimentally manipulated.

A complete-case approach was used: students were included if they had nonmissing values on all three variables. The final analytic sample included 253 students. The same sample was used for the correlation, regression, and exploratory mediation analyses to ensure that comparisons across models were based on identical observations.

Measures

Academic Performance

Academic performance was operationalized as GPA, recorded on a 0–4 scale in the dataset. GPA was treated as a continuous outcome variable in all regression analyses.

Weekday Bedtime

Average weekday bedtime was measured using the variable WeekdayBed. Bedtime was recorded in continuous hours, with 24 representing midnight and 25 representing 1:00 a.m. the following day. Higher values therefore indicated later bedtimes. For the quadratic regression, bedtime was centered at midnight by subtracting 24 from the original values. The centered variable and its squared term were included in the model.

Weekday Sleep Duration

Average weekday sleep duration was measured using the variable WeekdaySleep, expressed in hours per night. This variable was included as an additional predictor in the adjusted regression model and as a potential mediator in an exploratory mediation analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted in Python using pandas, NumPy, SciPy, and statsmodels. Figures were created using Matplotlib and seaborn. Descriptive statistics summarized the distributions of GPA, weekday bedtime, and weekday sleep duration. A scatterplot with a linear regression line and a locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) curve was used to visually examine the form of the bedtime–GPA association.

Pearson’s correlation was calculated to assess the bivariate association between weekday bedtime and GPA. A simple ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was then fitted with GPA as the outcome and weekday bedtime as the predictor. To explore potential curvature, a quadratic model containing midnight-centered bedtime and its squared term was fitted. A nested-model F test examined whether adding the quadratic term improved model fit relative to the linear model.

Diagnostics for the simple linear regression included a residuals-versus-fitted plot to assess linearity and homoscedasticity, a normal Q–Q plot to assess the residual distribution, and an influence plot displaying leverage, externally studentized residuals, and Cook’s distance. These diagnostics were used to identify observations warranting further examination; observations were not excluded on the basis of these diagnostics.

To examine whether the bedtime–GPA association changed after accounting for sleep duration, a multiple regression model included both weekday bedtime and weekday sleep duration as predictors. The bedtime coefficient and explained variance were compared with those from the unadjusted model. For visualization, adjusted predictions were calculated with sleep duration held at its sample mean, and 95% confidence intervals for the predicted mean GPA were displayed.

Finally, an exploratory mediation analysis estimated the indirect association between weekday bedtime and GPA involving weekday sleep duration. Path a was estimated by regressing sleep duration on bedtime. Path b and the adjusted bedtime association, c′, were estimated by regressing GPA on both bedtime and sleep duration. The indirect association was calculated as the product ab.

An approximate 95% Monte Carlo confidence interval for ab was obtained from 100,000 simulated coefficient pairs. Coefficients were sampled independently from normal distributions centered on their OLS estimates, with standard deviations equal to their estimated standard errors. This procedure assumed zero sampling covariance between the estimates of a and b. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated products defined the confidence interval. A random seed of 2026 was used for reproducibility.

Regression inference used conventional OLS standard errors. Statistical tests were two-sided with an alpha level of .05, and regression results were reported using unstandardized coefficients and 95% confidence intervals. The quadratic and mediation analyses were treated as exploratory. Because the data were observational, the estimated associations were not interpreted as evidence of causal effects or a causal mediation mechanism.

def show_equation(title, symbolic, model, outcome, terms):
    display(Markdown(f"**{title}（N = {int(model.nobs)}）**"))
    display(Math(symbolic))

    equation = outcome + f" = {model.params['Intercept']:.4f}"

    for parameter, variable in terms:
        coefficient = model.params[parameter]
        sign = "+" if coefficient >= 0 else "-"
        equation += (
            rf" {sign} {abs(coefficient):.4f}\,{variable}"
        )

    display(Math(equation))

Results and General Discussion

中文

在 253 名学生中，较晚的工作日就寝时间与较低 GPA 存在较弱的负关联。二次回归未提供支持弯曲关系的充分证据，因此无法据此识别最佳就寝时间。控制工作日睡眠时长后，就寝时间与 GPA 的负关联增强，但模型仍仅解释约 5.2% 的 GPA 方差。探索性路径分析显示，睡眠时长对应的正向间接关联与负向直接关联方向相反，呈现抑制模式。整体而言，就寝时间和睡眠时长与 GPA 的关系较为复杂，这些观察性结果不足以得出改变就寝时间或睡眠时长会导致 GPA 改变的结论。

English

Among 253 students, later weekday bedtime was weakly associated with lower GPA. Quadratic regression provided insufficient evidence of curvature, preventing identification of an optimal bedtime. The negative association between bedtime and GPA became stronger after adjusting for weekday sleep duration, although the model explained only approximately 5.2% of GPA variance. Exploratory path analysis identified a positive indirect association opposing the negative direct association, consistent with a suppression pattern. Overall, the associations among bedtime, sleep duration, and GPA were complex, and these observational findings do not establish that changing bedtime or sleep duration would cause changes in GPA.

The exploratory path analysis did not support the simple explanation that later bedtime was associated with lower GPA through shorter sleep duration. Although later bedtime was associated with shorter sleep duration, sleep duration was negatively associated with GPA after adjustment for bedtime. The resulting positive indirect association opposed the negative adjusted bedtime association, producing a pattern consistent with statistical suppression. This pattern helps explain why the bedtime coefficient became more negative after sleep duration was included in the model.

Importantly, the negative adjusted coefficient for sleep duration should not be interpreted as evidence that reducing sleep would improve academic performance. It represents a conditional association among students with the same modeled bedtime and may reflect unmeasured differences in health, schedules, or other characteristics. The present design cannot distinguish among these explanations.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the analyses were observational and did not establish the temporal ordering of bedtime, sleep duration, and academic performance. Although the original study collected sleep diaries over a two-week period, the present analyses used student-level averages rather than examining changes over time (Onyper et al., 2012). Consequently, the findings cannot establish that later bedtimes lead to lower GPA. Reverse causation is also possible: academic difficulties or greater academic demands may influence students’ sleep schedules.

Future research could address this limitation through a longitudinal design that follows students across an academic semester or multiple semesters. For example, Creswell et al. (2023) found that sleep duration measured early in the academic term predicted end-of-term GPA after accounting for previous-term GPA. Building on this approach, future studies could repeatedly measure bedtime, sleep duration, and academic outcomes to examine whether earlier sleep patterns predict subsequent performance. Repeated measurements could also distinguish differences between students from changes within the same student. However, temporal precedence alone would not eliminate confounding or establish causation.

Second, the present regression models included only bedtime and sleep duration. Factors such as chronotype, mental health, alcohol use, class schedules, and study habits may be associated with both sleep and academic performance. Previous research has examined several of these factors jointly, illustrating the broader context in which sleep–performance associations occur (Onyper et al., 2012). Future studies should identify relevant confounders using a clearly specified theoretical model and assess prior academic achievement, rather than assuming that including more predictors necessarily improves causal inference.

Third, diary-based sleep measures may be affected by inaccurate reporting, and a two-week recording period may not represent students’ sleep throughout a semester. Future research could combine sleep diaries with actigraphy over longer periods, following approaches used in previous studies (Creswell et al., 2023; Okano et al., 2019). Average weekday bedtime also does not capture sleep quality, night-to-night variability, or differences between weekday and weekend schedules. Future studies could examine these dimensions separately. In particular, sleep regularity should be distinguished from social jetlag, which concerns differences in sleep timing between work or school days and free days. Previous research has associated greater social jetlag with poorer academic performance (Haraszti et al., 2014).

Fourth, the statistical findings require cautious interpretation. The models explained a relatively small proportion of GPA variance, and the nonsignificant quadratic term does not establish that the relationship is strictly linear or identify an optimal bedtime. Moreover, the exploratory mediation analysis should not be interpreted as evidence that sleep duration is a causal mechanism. Cross-sectional mediation estimates can differ substantially from the longitudinal processes they are intended to represent (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Future studies could use appropriately timed repeated measurements of bedtime, sleep duration, and academic performance, while accounting for prior values and relevant confounders.

The Monte Carlo confidence interval for the indirect association also relied on normal approximations for the path coefficients and an assumption of zero sampling covariance between them. Future analyses could examine robustness using participant-level bootstrap confidence intervals, heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors, and sensitivity analyses for influential observations. These procedures could assess statistical stability but would not resolve the causal limitations of the research design.

Finally, quasi-experimental designs could strengthen causal inference without deliberately requiring students to restrict sleep. For example, researchers could study an institutionally introduced change in class start times. A relevant precedent is the pre–post study by Dunster et al. (2018), which found that delayed school start times were associated with longer sleep and improved academic performance among high school students. However, those findings should not be assumed to generalize directly to college students.

A future college study could compare changes in sleep and academic outcomes between institutions or programs that delayed class start times and comparable groups whose schedules remained unchanged. A difference-in-differences analysis could evaluate whether outcomes changed differently between these groups. Credible causal interpretation would require attention to assumptions such as parallel trends and the absence of differential concurrent changes, supported by multiple observations before and after the policy change (Wing et al., 2018).

Importantly, this design would primarily estimate the consequences of a scheduling policy rather than the isolated effect of bedtime. Class schedules could influence attendance, study opportunities, and other behaviors in addition to sleep. Complementary randomized studies could evaluate voluntary sleep-support interventions without assigning students to harmful sleep deprivation. Together, these approaches could clarify whether improving sleep-related conditions produces meaningful changes in academic performance.
